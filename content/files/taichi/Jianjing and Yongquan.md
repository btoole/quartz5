Both are important acupuncture points, and in Tai Chi they have a useful **structural/body-awareness interpretation** beyond their traditional medical descriptions.

### Jianjing — 肩井 (GB21)

![[x-attachments/Pasted image 20260810092932.png|221]]![[x-attachments/Pasted image 20260810092953.png|149]]![[x-attachments/Pasted image 20260810093030.png|138]]

**[[Jianjing]] (肩井)** literally means **"Shoulder Well."**

- **Chinese:** 肩井
- **Pinyin:** _Jiānjǐng_
- **Acupuncture designation:** **GB21** — Gallbladder meridian
- **Location:** roughly at the highest point of the trapezius muscle, midway between the base of the neck and the lateral end of the shoulder.

In Tai Chi terminology, this is a particularly interesting location because it is associated with **releasing the shoulders downward**.

When you hear:

> **沉肩 — Chén jiān — "sink the shoulders"**

one useful way of experiencing it is to allow the area around Jianjing to soften and descend.

Importantly, **"sink" does not mean force the shoulders downward**. If you actively push them down, you create tension—the opposite of _song_ (鬆).

Instead:

**Crown rises → Jianjing releases → shoulders settle → elbows become heavy.**

That creates a very characteristic Tai Chi structural relationship.

### Yongquan — 湧泉 (KI1)

![[x-attachments/Pasted image 20260810094140.png|151]]![[x-attachments/Pasted image 20260810094155.png|191]]

**[[Yongquan]] (湧泉)** means **"Gushing Spring"** or **"Bubbling Spring."**

- **Chinese:** 湧泉
- **Pinyin:** _Yǒngquán_
- **Acupuncture designation:** **KI1** — Kidney 1, the first point of the Kidney meridian
- **Location:** on the sole of the foot, approximately in the depression in the upper/middle portion of the sole, between the second and third metatarsal regions.

The name is wonderfully evocative: **a spring rising from the earth.**

In Tai Chi, Yongquan is often used as an image for **rooting**.

But again, rooting doesn't mean pressing the feet into the floor.

Rather:

> **The foot receives the ground.**

The weight settles through the leg into the foot, while the body remains sufficiently relaxed that the foot can continuously adapt.

---

There is also a very elegant vertical relationship:

**Baihui (百会)**\
_Crown of the head_\
⬆️\
**Jianjing (肩井)**\
_Shoulder releases_\
⬇️\
**Dantian (丹田)**\
_Center / integration_\
⬇️\
**Yongquan (湧泉)**\
_Root / ground_

You could think of the Tai Chi body as having **two simultaneous tendencies**:

> **The crown rises.**\
> **The feet sink.**

And between them the body becomes suspended rather than compressed.

This is why the traditional instruction **虚领顶劲 — xū lǐng dǐng jìn** ("empty/spiritually lift the crown") is so important. The upward and downward vectors create a kind of **dynamic suspension**.

And this gives us a particularly nice way of understanding the earlier **"One Crown, Three Voids"** idea:

- **Crown:** light upward suspension
- **Shoulder/armpit region:** open and released
- **Palms:** empty/receptive
- **Soles/Yongquan:** rooted but not gripping

The result is not a rigid vertical column. It is more like a **tree**: roots downward, crown upward, and the trunk remains alive and responsive.

One small qualification: the exact location and interpretation of these points can vary somewhat among acupuncture and Tai Chi lineages. So I'd regard the descriptions above as **traditional body-mapping concepts**, rather than precise anatomical structures.
